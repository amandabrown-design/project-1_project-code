Paradise Garage

Why This Matters to Me

Dance is as old as humanity. People have always used their bodies as vessels to communicate the language of spirit. The dance culture of the late 1970s and 1980s matters to me because technology has transformed how we gather. Dancing used to be a place for communal, shame-free connection. Now partying is often watched, recorded, and performed, and joy can feel fabricated. The Paradise Garage shows what a dance floor can be: people together, with nothing to perform.

What Was the Paradise Garage?

The Paradise Garage was a members-only nightclub located in a former parking garage in lower Manhattan (84 King Street) It operated from 1977 to 1987. Larry Levan was the resident DJ, and the club was known for its elaborate hand-built custom sound system and all-night dancing. The crowd was largely Black, Latino, and gay. For many members, it was a place to be safe, seen, and free.

A Space for the Marginalized

House music grew from of Black and Latino LGBTQ nightlife. Its first home was the “Warehouse” in Chicago, which gave the genre its name. The Paradise Garage was part of the same culture in New York, and its sound became known as “garage.” These clubs were places where people excluded elsewhere could gather, dance, and belong. The music carried that purpose.

Timeline


  • 1977 — Michael Brody opens the Paradise Garage at 84 King Street, a former parking garage in lower Manhattan. Larry Levan is resident DJ.
  • 1978—The club runs as a members-only space, with dancing from Saturday night into Sunday. A custom sound system becomes a model for club audio.
  • 1981 — AIDS emerges in the US and devastates gay communities, including many Black and Latino men, while the government response lags. The Garage loses dancers, staff, and DJs, and the dance floor becomes a place to mourn as well as celebrate.
  • 1987 — The club closes. The final party is held in September 27, 1987.

Cultural Influence

The Garage helped shape house and garage music, and its influence spread quickly around the world. Corporate interests took notice. They copied the sound, distilled it for wider audiences, and repackaged the work of underground DJs for profit. This is how we came to know EDM and other genres, often with the Black and Latino LGBTQ communities who created them left out of the story. The Garage set the standard for club sound systems and established the DJ as someone who builds a night over hours rather than playing a set of hits. Its biggest legacy may be the idea that a club can be a community, one that still guides dance floors built on care and inclusion.
